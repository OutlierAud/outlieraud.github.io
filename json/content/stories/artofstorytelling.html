If data could speak, what would it say?
And if it could speak, would we understand it?

Would it speak in numbers, reveal itself through patterns, or tell its story through the relationships between seemingly unrelated things?

Would it reveal hidden patterns and truths, expose biases, challenge our assumptions, or surprise us with the unexpected?

Coming from a creative communications background, I’ve been curious about how I can draw on my experience to become a better data scientist. In this article, I’d like to explore the intersection of creative design and data science, and share some of my thoughts with you.

Bridging Art and Science

Done well, data visualisation can be a powerful communication tool. It helps analysts explore data, recognise patterns, and uncover insights that might otherwise go unnoticed. A carefully chosen visualisation can make a complex relationship easier to understand.

This is a new and exciting area for me. In my previous line of work, data visualisation had always been about presenting information to an audience in the best way possible. But now, the tables have turned. I am my own audience, and the director at the same time.

I'm floored by the plethora of tools at my disposal. While experimenting with different data visualisation techniques is exciting, it's important not to get carried away and focus on recognising when a particular method is or isn't suitable for the task at hand.

These are some of the questions I ask myself before choosing a data visualisation tool:

  • What kind of data type am I analysing?
  • What am I trying to analyse?
  • How do I want to the answer to be presented visually?

After all the hard work we’ve invested in finding answers, we arrive at the finale: crafting a story around what we’ve found, being careful not to put words in the data’s mouth.

Data is Fact. We are the Storytellers.

The data isn't in and of itself THE story. We are the ones crafting the narrative.

A dataset doesn’t decide which questions are worth asking, which patterns deserve attention, or which findings matter to an audience. Those decisions come from the person working with the data. Two people can analyse the same dataset and produce very different accounts of what it reveals.

The story actually begins before the first chart is drawn.
It begins with the questions we choose to ask.

Data Doesn’t Speak for Itself

We often hear that data tells a story. And just as we have good chefs and horrible chefs, we also have good storytellers and bad storytellers. The same data can be presented in ways that illuminate, confuse or mislead. How well we tell its story depends not only on our analytical skills, but also on the choices we make in communicating what we’ve found.

I always ask myself: Is the story complete, accurate, engaging and easy to understand? Or does it mislead or oversimplify? Or perhaps bore its audience to death. Worse yet, give the audience a splitting headache trying to make sense of it.

Data itself doesn’t speak. It’s the storyteller who gives it a voice.

Telling It Like It Is, Artfully

Telling a story through data requires a blend of both technical skills and creative storytelling abilities. It's more than just deciding color and font styles.

Say What?

Avoid sensory overload. Avoid unnecessary complexity. If your audience is overwhelmed by the amount of information presented, they will struggle to understand the story you're trying to tell. Keep things simple and focus on the key insights.

I know I'm excited to share every little insight I gained, but I also know that less is often more when it comes to data storytelling.

Understanding who my audience is helps me decide which vocabulary to use and how much detail to include. After all, I wouldn't explain a statistical concept to a fellow data scientist in quite the same way I'd explain it to someone with no technical background.

But simplicity has its limits, too. We shouldn't sacrifice important context or nuance just to make a story easier to digest. The challenge is to make complex information accessible without oversimplifying it. Ultimately, the story needs to make sense to the people we’re telling it to.

Direct The Audience's Attention

Every visual element competes for attention. How do we guide the viewer’s eye through the information in a natural, logical sequence? The size of a heading, the contrast between colours, the position of a chart, and even the amount of white space can influence what an audience notices first.

Some questions I ask myself:

  • What do I want my audience to see?
  • What information can take a back seat?
  • How do I make this part stand out to capture attention?
  • Am I giving the data room to breathe by using white space efficiently, or is the layout cluttered or awkwardly sparse?
  • Does my layout silently guide the audience's eye naturally? Or does it feel disorganised?

These are decisions I recognise from my years in creative communications. A well-designed layout guides the reader through information in a deliberate order, helping them distinguish the main message from the supporting details.

But there's a responsibility that comes with this ability to direct attention.

Emphasising one finding while burying another can change how a story is interpreted. We need to be mindful of what we highlight, what we leave in the background, and whether our choices give the audience a fair understanding of the evidence.

 

Deliberate & Functional Design

Consistency in layout, headings, typography and colour helps an audience navigate information without having to work out a new visual system at every turn.

Imagine reading a book printed horizontally, with a few vertically aligned pages popping up now and then for no apparent reason. That would throw you off and cause considerable frustration!

The same principle applies to data visualisation. Design choices should serve a purpose, not simply decorate the page. Colour can distinguish categories or draw attention to an important finding. Position can make comparisons easier. Labels can provide context that might otherwise be missed.

Honesty, The Best Policy

And of course, good design shouldn't come at the expense of accuracy. A visually striking chart that obscures the scale, distorts a comparison or exaggerates a difference isn't doing its job, no matter how beautiful it looks.
