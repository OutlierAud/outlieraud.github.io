Ever feel like you’re not good enough, even when you are? This one's a little more personal. I'm taking a closer look at my own self doubt while pondering whether imposter syndrome might explain some of it. Because God knows, we all have enough to worry about without doubting ourselves constantly.

The more I learn, the less I know

There’s something rather unsettling about learning a new discipline and discovering that the more you learn, the less you seem to know.

That’s been my experience with data science.

When I started my Master’s, I knew I had a lot to learn. That’s hardly a revelation when you’re embarking on a new field of study, but I expected that as I acquired new knowledge and skills, I’d gradually feel more equipped to put them to use.

And in many ways, I do feel more equipped.

I’m learning to work with data, apply statistical methods, write code, and make sense of information that can help answer meaningful questions. My technical toolkit is slowly growing with every course, article or project I complete.

Yet, the more I learn about data science, the more I become aware of how much I still don’t know. What initially seemed manageable becomes more complicated as I begin to understand the nuances, the assumptions, the limitations, and the sheer breadth of the discipline.

And instead of feeling increasingly prepared to enter the field, I sometimes find myself wondering whether I’ll ever know enough.

Is it imposter syndrome?

At what point does learning translate into feeling competent? And how do you know when you’re ready?

The subject came up in one of my courses at the University of Colorado Boulder, where the lecturer, Dr. Jane Wall, who helped develop the university’s data science programme, raised the topic of imposter syndrome. An accompanying article got me thinking about whether the concept might explain some of what I’ve been experiencing.

Imposter syndrome, or imposter phenomenon, describes a pattern of doubting one’s abilities and struggling to internalise one’s accomplishments, even when there is evidence of competence.

It’s an interesting concept, particularly in a field like data science, where the learning never really ends.

I’m still developing my technical skills and I haven’t spent years working as a data scientist. Understanding a concept in a classroom and being able to apply it independently to an unfamiliar, real-world problem are, in my experience, two completely different things.

These are genuine considerations when preparing for a new career.

So, is it imposter syndrome if you recognise that you still have a great deal to learn? Or is it simply an honest assessment of where you are in your professional development?

Perhaps the more useful question is whether I’m assessing my abilities accurately, or allowing the things I don’t yet know to overshadow the things I do.

I must recognise that I need more statistical knowledge and still lack professional experience in the field without dismissing the knowledge I’ve gained through my studies.

The challenge is learning to distinguish between the two.

But there’s something else that makes this transition particularly challenging for me.

When experience becomes a double-edged sword

I’m not new to the working world. I’ve spent more than two decades in creative multimedia development, branding, project management, and client relationships. I’ve helped build and expand businesses, managed projects, worked with teams, and made decisions that required sound judgement, experience, and acute problem-solving skills.

I know what it feels like to be competent at something.

And I think that’s part of what makes being a beginner again so uncomfortable.

In my previous work, experience gave me a foundation from which to make decisions confidently. I didn’t need to know everything to know what questions to ask, anticipate problems, or work out how to get things done.

In data science, I’m developing that foundation all over again. While I’m not entirely new to the technical world due to my background in multimedia computing, data science is a different discipline, and there’s still a great deal for me to learn, particularly in statistics and analytical methods. In some ways, I’m building on familiar ground while learning to navigate unfamiliar territory.

Just as my professional experience doesn’t automatically translate into technical expertise in this field, learning a new discipline doesn’t erase the experience I’ve already accumulated.

I can be an experienced professional and a beginner at data science at the same time. The trick is learning to be comfortable with both.

Perhaps this is one of the less discussed challenges of changing careers. When you’ve spent years building expertise in one field, you become accustomed to a certain level of confidence in your abilities. Starting over means accepting that you won’t immediately have the same fluency or independence in your new discipline.

You have to give yourself permission to learn again, including the uncomfortable parts.

Why does data science seem to invite so much self doubt?

My take on this is that perhaps data science is particularly good at reminding us of how much we don’t know. It’s a broad and demanding discipline that draws on statistics, mathematics, programming, and domain knowledge, with new tools and techniques continually emerging. Just as you begin to feel comfortable with one area, you discover another that demands your attention.

It’s not difficult to see how this can leave you questioning whether you know enough, or whether you’ll ever feel ready.

Still, there’s a distinction worth making here. It’s one thing to recognise that the discipline is demanding and acknowledge genuine gaps in my knowledge and skills that I need to address. It’s another to attribute every instance of self doubt to imposter syndrome.

The goalposts keep moving

I suspect there’s another reason I sometimes struggle to feel that I’m making enough progress: the more I learn, the higher I raise the bar for myself.

When I understand something that once seemed difficult, I don’t always stop to acknowledge my progress. My attention shifts to the next concept I haven’t mastered, the next skill I need to develop, or the next thing I discover I don’t yet know.

The finish line just keeps moving!

Of course, ambition and a willingness to keep learning are valuable. I don’t want to become complacent, and I have no illusions about how much work it takes to develop technical competence. In fact, I can't even see the finish line, and it makes me nervous to think that I might never get there. But I also need to be careful not to let that ambition become a source of self doubt.

I think I need to recognise that there’s a difference between maintaining high standards and setting standards that make it impossible to recognise my own progress.

If I only measure myself against everything I have yet to learn, I’ll always come up short. There will always be another skill to develop, another problem I haven’t encountered, or someone who knows more than I do.

That doesn’t mean my shortcomings aren’t real and just imposter syndrome at play. It means they aren’t the whole picture.

And perhaps I need to pay as much attention to what I can do as to what I cannot. Not to inflate my abilities, but to recognise them more fairly.

It seems a reasonable principle to apply to myself, particularly when I’m learning a discipline that places so much emphasis on evidence, critical thinking, and drawing conclusions that the available information can actually support.

Keeping self doubt in check

I’m not convinced that all self doubt is a bad thing. Sometimes it serves a purpose. It drives us to examine our assumptions, identify gaps in our knowledge, and recognise when we need more practice or a different approach.

The trouble starts when we allow those doubts to define our abilities or potential. There’s a difference between saying, “I don’t know how to do this yet,” and saying, “I’m not capable of doing this.” The first identifies a gap that can be addressed while the second draws a conclusion about ourselves that we may not have enough evidence to support.

When I feel unprepared, perhaps I need to be more specific about what I’m missing. Is it a particular technical skill? More practice applying a method independently? Experience working with unfamiliar data? These are concrete things I can work towards.

And when I feel that I’ve accomplished too little, perhaps I need to look back at where I started rather than continually comparing myself with where I want to be.

I don’t need to convince myself that I’m an expert in spatial analysis or fluent in statistics. I just need to recognise that my skills and knowledge can be enough, even if they aren't everything I would like them to be. Nor do I need to pretend that every doubt is irrational.

I simply need to recognise that I’m still learning, that progress takes time, and that not being where I want to be yet doesn’t mean I won’t get there.

A note to my future self

Perhaps I’ll revisit this piece one day and smile at the questions I had when I wrote it. I hope that by then, I'd have gained the experience and confidence to call myself a data scientist without feeling the need to qualify it.

Not because I’ll know everything. I doubt anyone does!

But because I'd have developed the skills, judgement, and experience to recognise what I bring to the table, acknowledge what I still need to learn, and feel comfortable with both.

Until then, there’s work to be done, things to learn, and plenty of room to grow.

And God knows, I have enough to worry about without making the journey harder by doubting myself at every turn.
